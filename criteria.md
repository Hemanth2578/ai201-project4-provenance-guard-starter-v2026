# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. _"The
detector is accurate"_ is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. _"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"_ is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. _"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"_ is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area            | A question it could answer                                    |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI?                |
| Score spread    | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage  | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable?         |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded?  |
| Bad input       | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous?      |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---

## 1. Plain-style human writing is rarely accused

Of 8 human-written pieces in a plain style (short sentences, everyday
vocabulary, written by people writing in a second language or writing simply
on purpose), at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI in any single trial.

**Why this target:**
Signal one scores predictable text as machine-like, and plain writing is
predictable, so these are the writers my system is most likely to accuse, and
the ones least able to argue back. Zero would be the honest ideal, but one
model-driven misfire in 8 is the most I can accept before the label stops
meaning anything for these writers. Two would mean a quarter of them could be
accused in a bad run.

---

## 2. Ordinary human writing gets a clean result

Of 10 ordinary human-written pieces (essays, emails and posts written without
any AI help, not drawn from criterion 1's set), at least 7 are labelled
high-confidence human in every trial. "Unsure" counts as a miss.

**Why this target:**
A writer who sees "unsure" on their own work still has to defend it, so a clean
"human" result is what a real writer actually needs. I leave room for 3 because
the unsure band exists to absorb genuine doubt, and some ordinary writing will
fall into it. If more than 3 of 10 ordinary pieces land outside "human", the
band is catching ordinary writers, not doubtful cases.

---

## 3. AI and human writing score well apart

The average combined score of 8 clearly-AI pieces (generated by a chatbot from
a one-line prompt, unedited) is at least 0.30 higher than the average combined
score of 8 clearly-human pieces (the first 8 of criterion 2's set), in every
trial.

**Why this target:**
0.30 is the width of my unsure band (0.35 to 0.65). A smaller gap means the
two group averages could both sit inside "unsure", so the system would not be
telling even the easiest cases apart. I state it as a fixed 0.30, not as "the
band width", so the target does not move if I change thresholds in unit 8.

---

## 4. Every label is reachable with real text

For each of the three labels, at least 2 inputs, chosen and written down
before the first run, produce that label in all 3 trials. The "unsure" inputs
are AI-generated pieces with roughly half their sentences rewritten by hand.

**Why this target:**
One input per label could be a fluke; two shows the label covers a real range
of text, not a single lucky score. All 3 trials, because a writer who
resubmits the same work and gets a different verdict has every reason not to
trust any of them. Choosing the inputs in advance stops me trying texts until
one happens to land.

---

## 5. Long text is never scored in secret

Any submission longer than 250 words is rejected with status 413 and a message
stating the limit, and the rejection appears in the audit log. This holds for
all of: 251 words, 1,000 words and 10,000 words. A 249-word submission is
scored normally.

**Why this target:**
The detector reads at most 400 tokens and silently drops the rest, so a writer
could be judged on an opening they did not write, such as a quotation, while
the rest of their work is ignored. 400 tokens is roughly 300 words; 250 leaves
a margin for punctuation-heavy text, which uses more tokens per word. Word
count can be checked in the route before the model runs. Rejecting is
inconvenient for long work, but being judged on part of it is worse.
